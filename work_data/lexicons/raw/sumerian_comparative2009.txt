INTRODUCTION 


In the Dictionary which follows we set up Niger-Congo etymologies for Sumerian. These 
run into the hundreds and embrace many of the most common Sumerian words. In the 


face of this overwhelming evidence, Sumerian can no longer be regarded as an isolate. 


Greenberg in Languages of Africa (1973) points out on page 32 that “of 50 nouns, 
taken at random from Johnston’s study of Bantu, 21 were referable without trouble to 
Proto-West Sudanic. Of these same 50 nouns in English only 17 could be traced to 
Proto-Indo-European.” Bernal (1991 : 47), likewise, has pointed out that 50% of the 
Greek vocabulary and 80% of Greek proper names lack Indo-European or Anatolian 


etymologies. By contrast Sumerian’s affinity to Niger-Congo is beyond doubt. 


That is not to say that the “facts” are invariably straightforward or undisputed. 
Greenberg himself has been subjected to much criticism. The remarks about etymology 
on the front page, by Guiraud and Casares, are counsels of perfection. We are dealing 
with a huge time span, involving great changes between man and his environment, and 
vast distances that divide the languages studied. The gaps in our knowledge are greater 
than what is known. And comparative linguistics, which is an exact science, is based on 
etymology, which is an art. Changes in meaning remain difficult to control scientifically 
(Guiraud 1967 : 23, map). Guiraud recommends combining historical with structural 


methods. We have the data. But do we have the imagination and method to exploit it ? 


Weinreich (1967) rightly says that compilers of dictionaries, in general, have an 
“approach” rather than a “method”, “because lexicography itself uses many methods, 
none of which have been fully explained.” In the case of etymology we at least know 
what we are aiming at, but also realise our limitations. The reconstruction of the details 
of past civilisations over thousands of years can only be partial, at best. We try to enter 
the minds of men who inhabited a world that we do not know, with a mindset formed by 
superstition and magic. Their reality was different. So was their response to it. But the 
languages that we speak today are the legacy of this remote and mysterious past, and stll 


carry features that find their explanation in it.

RECONSTRUCTED ROOTS 


The early comparativists concentrated on the abstract root V that underlay groups of cognates. 


That was the goal of their reconstructions. Chantraine in his Dictionnaire étymologique de la 


langue _grecque (1968) discusses the problems involved in Greek etymology. In this 
connection he mentions Benveniste’s (1935) theory of a three-letter Indo-European root 
(CVC). This resembles a common Sumerian pattern, and also recalls Westermann’s CV(C) 
“stem words”. Root theorists tend to assume the root was a verb. We prefer to treat the 


“noun” as primary (See our Sumerian Comparative Grammar). The Indoeuropeanist Hirt used 


“bases”, which were nouns. Westermann’s “Stammwé6rterbuch” sets up alternative 


“reconstructions” (not starred forms) which can often be identified as real original words. 


Chantraine sees the root as the ultimate source of Indo-European words. We propose a Proto- 
Niger-Congo CVCVCV word, with a CV root (really an old stem word) framed by a CV 


prefix and a CV suffix. Westermann’s (1927) typical Proto-Western Sudanic “Stammwéorter” 


have the structure CV or CV(C), a three-letter root. But the third letter is amissable (like 
Sumerian final consonants), and was once part of a suffix. We take the Indo-European 
languages, like Sumerian, ultimately back to Proto-Niger-Congo. In setting up Sumerian 


etymologies, we concentrate, like Westermann, on the root, rather than prefixes or suffixes. 


Where we cite PWS or PWN monosyllables however, we are assuming that at a more remote 
date there was an African language with free word order, made up of CV monosyllables. The 
—C which optionally follows these monosyllables (eg in Westermann) tends to vary. In other 
words it does not necessarily belong to the original CV root, but to a suffix. The same 
situation applies in Sumerian. Thus CVC is from CV-C(V) by reduction (as in many Niger- 


Congo languages). 


The Sumerian initial consonant may be lost, CV > V; or it may be preserved in a repeated 
following —C if there was original reduplication, once very common. Eg Sumerian an “sky”, 
from *nana (PWS na “above”). When a word is reduced to V severe problems may arise in 
identifying the lost initial consonant. Thus is Sumerian a “water” from (m)a “water”, (r)a 
“water” or from PWN KWAK “wash”, PWN GWAL “το flow”, PWS gia “water” (with lenis 
labiovelar) ? Or even from PWS bd “swamp” ? We know b > w > #in Sumerian. Johnston 
(1922 : 411) gives δ, nja “water” from the Kwango-Kasai area (Congo). And Sumerian also 
uses e for “canal, irrigation ditch”. Sumerian nj = ny can be shown to be lost, eg in nyi > i 


“hand”. But does this prove this was the origin ? 


The Sumerian monosyllable, unlike our remote PrePNC hypothetical CV monosyllables, has 
been reduced from a Niger-Congo polysyllabic word of the form CVCVCV. The ultimate 
historical process is : *CV + *CV + *CV > *CVCVCV > (C)V(C). The last stage tends to 
represent historical Sumerian. Often this reduction can be demonstrated to have happened. 
We generally are left with the Sumerian root, or a vestige of it, to represent the word. 
Westermann’s CV(C) bears a remarkable resemblance to the typical Sumerian root word 
CV(C). But as we have seen VC and V can be explained likewise as from CV(C) < 


CVCVCV. Mande too can be shown to have once had noun prefixes. 


The monosyllabic CV roots which we posit as the source of CVCVCV structures predate 
Proto-Niger-Congo and cannot strictly be classified, in any modern sense, as nouns or verbs. 
Structurally they are more akin to nouns. They appear to come mostly from concrete nouns 


(body parts). Their genesis lies beyond the historical horizon. 


LOST LARYNGEALS 

In his introduction Chantraine briefly alludes to Indo-European “laryngeals”, (attested in 
Hittite as h, but generally lost in other Indo-European languages), which we have explained 
elsewhere (Campbell-Dunn 2008) as remnants of old Niger-Congo CV prefixes. We regard 
the glottal stop onset of German words with initial vowel as further evidence for Niger-Congo 
CV prefixes in Indo-European. The glottal stop reflects a reduced consonant. Prefixes and 
suffixes alike tend to be eroded and lost due to weak accentuation. In Sumerian old prefixes 
tend to be reduced to meaningless vowels; old suffixes tend to be reduced to a consonant, 


which becomes meaningless, weakens and is attached to the preceding CV root.. 


The Hittite “laryngeal” h is traceable to weakened Niger-Congo consonants (often from a 
prefix), but lost in most IE languages. See Lehmann (1955), Keiler (1970) etc for the IE 
details. Sumerian ἢ is from PNC k, as in hada 2 “brilliant”, ha-ra-an “road”, where ha = *ka. 
In many ways it resembles Hittite A . It is a remnant. Often it can be omitted, leaving a bare 
initial vowel (ara “road’’). So it must be a weak fricative, like the various Indo-European 
“laryngeals”. Compare the Niger-Congo prefixes in ἢ, the Khoisan ἢ of Hazda etc. Laryngeal 


Theory grew out of the work by De Saussure (1887) on vocalic coefficients. 


OLD PREFIXES 

Other morphological evidence for old Niger-Congo prefixes includes the augment on verbs in 
Greek, Sanskrit (abhar-) and Armenian (eber), which is also found in Bantu. Sumerian aka 
“make” has a possible survival of this. Also Sumerian dd “send”, (ad-da 4 “send”, PWS ta 
“go”), Sumerian dg “measure” (aga 2, aka 2 “measure”). Compare also Sumerian enim 
“word”, with e-. In Latin this element can be seen in a-mare “to love”, where it is similarly 
without function. This prefix once meant “go” or the like, and is also related to the prothetic 
vowel on nouns such as Greek astér, Armenian astt “star”. Historically it is a nominal feature. 
See Westermann (1927 : 179) for Mende i- (sg. poss. pron.) and other possible prefix 


remnants in Mande. Mande usually loses the noun prefixes. Sumerian has ib, ibi, ibila 3 etc.

REDUPLICATION 

Often there is doubt about whether an initial vowel comes from a reduced prefix, or an eroded 
reduplication. Reduplication is one of the earliest methods of morphology. Its history is 
closely connected with the history of the Niger-Congo prefixes. If the initial vowel matches 
the root vowel there must always be a suspicion that we have to do with an old reduplication. 
This is particularly relevant to Sumerian as it is to Mande (which has lost its prefixes), but 
often has like vowels in successive syllables. . Thus of the examples in the previous paragraph 
Sumerian ama may be from *mama. But a-da-al probably has a- as a prefix because —da-al 
may be from *dada-l. The last example Sumerian alim however, has a different root vowel 
which makes the a- unequivocally a prefix. Sumerian a-ba, a-na however might well come 
from reduplicated forms. Sumerian ara “road” partially matches Mande sira “road”. In 


Niger-Congo the first vowel of a reduplication was stressed ; prefixes lacked stress. 


OLD SUFFIXES 

Sumerian also preserves traces of Niger-Congo suffixes, such as —al, -l(a), -a (the article), 
demonstrative —/(i), -d(i) (the definite) and plurals —r(i), -n(i) seen in Mande. These suffixes 
are reduced (usually) to a final consonant. This distinguishes them from words with a second 
root, which tends to preserve full consonant + vowel. The problematic final —g on some 
Sumerian words is a very ancient plural —k, -ki meaning “many”. It occurs in Mande 
(Westermann 1927 : 163, 3) and on Mande tribal names : Malinke, Soninke; Benue-Congo 
Baloki, Bunaki, Gingwak; Atlantic Bedik; also Aramanik (Nilo-Saharan). So Sumerian dug 
“knee(s)” (2), “limbs” (pl.). The suffixes are typically weak demonstratives, articles, definites 
and related forms which were enclitic and lacked an accent of their own. In practice it is 
difficult to prove the exact meaning of many of these elements in Sumerian. But Mande is 
always a useful point of reference for identifying them. It is the closest branch of Niger- 


Congo to Sumerian. See Westermann (1927 : 162 — 167) for their meanings in Mande. 


Niger-Congo Gur “man” matches Sumerian gid “warrior, hero”, from PWS kid “man”. The 
unusual PWS accentuation (two successive acutes) shows the Stammwort is telescoped from 
two elements : ku “male, big” and la, da, ra “article” (or */a “hand”), compare PWS kuni, 
(kunu), (kudi) etc. Mande has kyé “man” < PWS kid. In Linear A the word is QA = kwa. 


Cretan placenames include Gournia. A Gur dialect is called Gurma.

VOWELS & VOWEL GRADES 

Westermann sets up three original vowels for early PWS : a, i, u. From these he derives 
secondary e and o. His commonest changes are : i > e, u > o. But a can change to both e and 
o (which recalls the problems regarding these vowels in Indo-European). Also common are 
the changes ia > e, ua > o. So the vocalic, like the consonantal system, becomes more 
complex. Secondary e and o are vowel grades. But in PWS the secondary e and o are poorly 
established as phonemes and relatively infrequent. Often they are little more than 


nondistinctive allophones. Mukarovsky has much the same protovocalic system. 


Sumerian does not have graphs with o. But there is sufficient evidence that o was written as u. 
Nasalised vowels are also common in the Niger-Congo languages. One suspects these in 
Sumerian. An important PWS feature is i/u alternation : ti, tu etc. Most of these vocalisms can 
be demonstrated for Sumerian. Like Proto-Niger-Congo Sumerian has no diphthongs. We 
also have to do with vowel loss, initially, finally, sometimes medially, in Sumerian as in 
Niger-Congo. Guthrie (1971) reconstructed long vowels for Common Bantu. These appear to 
be contractions of like vowels. There is some evidence in Sumerian that double vowel 


spellings show a long vowel. 


Many Niger-Congo languages have secondary vowels : front with lip rounding, back without 
lip rounding. These developments have greatly increased the number of vowels in some 
languages. They may have occurred in Sumerian, but are unlikely to have been phonemic. 


The alien writing system does not record them. 


Niger-Congo languages also have nasalised vowels. We suspect these for Sumerian before 
various amissable consonants, which may sometimes have acted as nasalisation marks. 


Delafosse gives nasalised vowels for Mande. 


ACCENTS 

In Sumerian the root must have had initial syllable stress, with lesser stress on the second 
syllable, which prevented erosion. Sumerian stress never distinguishes different words. This 
main stress usually coincides with Niger-Congo original high pitch. Some Sudanic languages 
behave in the same way. They are primarily tone languages. Tones have been suggested for 
Sumerian (Diakonoff). They are likely, but definite evidence doesn’t exist, as they are not 
indicated by the script. It is worth mentioning that pitch accents appear to be marked on the 
Phaistos Disk, on Minoan inscribed cups, and possibly in Luvian. I know of no evidence for 


such marks in Sumerian. In Niger-Congo native scripts they are sporadic and optional. 


PELASGIAN 

Chantraine also devotes space to the matter of Pelasgian. Many words in Indo-European 
languages cannot be given Indo-European etymologies. Often they appear to be “Pelasgian”, 
Mediterranean substrate, general Aegean words etc. We take Pelasgian as referring to the 
presence of early African languages spoken around the Mediterranean before the coming of 
the Indo-Europeans. Many of the problematic items of vocabulary, loanwords of obscure 
origin etc, belong to this early stratum. These words are connected to early non-Indo- 
European (African) placenames such as Komo, Bari, Menidi etc. These are predominantly 
Niger-Congo and Nilo-Saharan. Pelasgian refers to surviving African substrate in Indo- 


European speaking areas. 


THE WIDER CONTEXT 

This book is about Sumerian, but Sumerian should be seen now in an extended African-cum- 
“Mediterranean” context. It is an African type language, with Indo-European (and other) 
connections. The following word list shows that PWS and PWN roots of CV form give the 
best comparisons. The meanings of these are important, but not to be taken too literally. Our 
“parts of speech” did not yet exist. Noun is identified with “action of noun” etc. We lack a 


notation that is appropriate. In some cases Sumerian words include more than one root. 


Often there is a Sumerian final consonant, which may preserve part of an old suffix. But 
such suffixes are no longer vital. The consonant has become an opaque addition to the root. 
Or the second part may be lost. See nos 15 & 21 on the list below. Thus *kuani “bird” 
becomes Sumerian hu, u. And *bwatu becomes ma. The Sumerian word eme “tongue” 
corresponds to Mande πὸ, né-nde “tongue”. Mande loss of the Niger-Congo syllable -me (de- 
me) shows that the de- carried the accent. For “go’”Mande has se ge-ré, with se (si) “foot” 
preceding however. This se is from PWS ta “to go’. (or PWS τὸ “immediately” ?). Sumerian 


giriz “foot” is from PNC *ki “go”. 


There also may be survivals of old prefixes. Again the meaning of these is often unclear (ib, 
ub “angle”). It may be difficult to prove that these are prefixes rather than eroded 
reduplications (ubur “breast” etc). Since both ultimately come from independent roots the 
distinction is to some extent illusory. In many ways Sumerian resembles Mande in its basic 
plan. Mande eliminates the Niger-Congo prefixes, but frequently, like Sumerian, combines

The whole of Niger-Congo may be treated as a complex diasystem. 


At times the original sound, even where it produces a Sumerian reflex, is in doubt. It appears 
that PNC once distinguished voiceless and voiced plosives, but the distinction was eroded by 
prenasalisation, which caused voicing. of voiceless consonants. (Compare the failure to 
distinguish B/P, G/K in Linear A). An example of this can be seen in Sumerian Jag “morsel”, 
but also Jag, with prenasalised g [yg]. We give what evidence from we have (from 
reconstructions & reflexes). Niger-Congo likewise, may show voiceless alongside voiced 
variants, eg Bantu di,ad “wear”, duk “plait”, Bantu ting “plait”, PWS Ia, (du) “weave”, tu “to 


carry, wear”. 


Different stages of the same word (doublets) may coexist : Sumerian aka,, ak, ag, as “make”. 
Is a- of a-ka a prefix or a reduced root ? Compare Sumerian ka, ka-ka “mouth”, ka 
“declaration”, (δ ka “gate” = “mouth”), but also ab “opening, window etc” ( pd “to open” 

= b ; PWN BADO “door”’). PWN has KA “home” (with door or gate), Bangi ekuki “gate”, 
Ngala engambi “gate”, but PWS ka “open, close”, PWS also gud “gate” (mouth), gua “hand, 
arm”, PWS kua “leg, foot”. All these are related. But the primary form is ΚΑ, which must 
have been a concrete noun, probably HAND. The gu- was a prefix, with loss of following *k. 
There is simplicity here, but great complexity in the way it is applied. One should also be 


aware of PWS pi “house”, PWN BUDA, BHULA “door”. Bantu has pito, bedo “door”. 


Fluctuation of sounds appears to occur. 


As is common in etymological dictionaries, there may be semantic slippage, or changes to the 
precise meaning of words. In part this is due to comparing words that have (or once had) 
different suffixes and prefixes. In part it is due to words changing their meaning over time. To 
insist on exact replications of meaning in comparisons is futile. Old basic nouns generally 
hold their meaning almost unchanged. So these are very important. Etymology aspires to be a 
science, but in many ways it remains an art. It aims to “resolve words into their root and 
secondary elements” (Casares 1969 : 30) by applying grammatical criteria, and controlling 
cognates with sound laws. But in practice matters may not be so straightforward. Uriel 


Weinreich’s strictures are a counsel of perfection, an ideal seldom completely attained. 


Some Sumerian words are much more productive than others. We mention among these a 
WATER, ώά ARM, STRENGTH, aka, ag, ak MAKE, an SKY, dui; , dug 4 SPEAK, é 
HOUSE, gi REED, gud, gu 4 BULL, gar, gar, g4 STORE, gis, gis, ges WOOD, igi EYE, 
FACE, ki LAND, li MAN, mda BOAT, me, mi, mi BE, SPEAK, nam RESPONSIBILITY, nig 
THING, sag HEAD, MAN, δὰ, Sag 4 HEART, Se GRAIN, su HAND, us, ud DAY, TIME. 
These all have over half a page of associated compounds (elements added after) in Bord’s 
Petit _Lexique. They all take Niger-Congo etymologies. Most are nouns. That they are 
compounded by postaddition suggests they were all once felt as nouns. Some are greatly 
reduced by phonetic attrition. Reduction to monosyllables is a regular treatment of common 
words. But reduction to vowels only (WATER, ARM, SPEAK, DAY) is linguistically 
anomalous. We have V (4), CV (13), CVC (11), VC (3), VCV (1). But the orthography may 
be misleading. Kwa (Ga, Ada) has wa “be strong” which would give Sumerian d “arm 
strength” by w-loss (from earlier ba “arm” ?) These common Sumerian words are not typical 
of Mande. One suspects Sumerian separated from Niger-Congo at a very early date when it 


was structurally more like Mande. Mande may represent the earliest stratum of Niger-Congo.

PHONETIC TRANSCRIPTION 


Phonetic symbols are as in our Sumerian Comparative Grammar. Thus subscript and 


superscript dots & | commas have been placed after the consonant. After plosives dots 
indicate retroflex, but n. may transcribe the velar nasal 7. Non-italic consonants are implosive. 
Greek letters and underlined letters indicate fricatives. In general I follow the notation of my 
sources wherever possible. The signs /, // etc are Khoisan implosives (“clicks”) as in 
Greenberg). The italic ? represents a glottal stop. A single quote ater a consonant means 
“palatalised”, before a consonant it indicates “lenis”. N represents “nasal consonant”. C is 


“consonant”, V is “vowel”.

THE COMPARATIVE DICTIONARY 


The entries consist of : 


I. 

The English meaning of the Sumerian word underlined as a rubric (the lemma). These 
meanings are in alphabetical order and are the reference structure behind the comparative 
dictionary. To the right comes the Sumerian word, together with other relevant Sumerian 


words listed below it. 


Il. 
A CV root (or roots) in bold capitals, indicating the hypothetical original Proto-Niger-Congo 
root (sometimes with alternatives). This is followed by suggested meanings of the original 


root and meanings which have developed from it at an early stage enclosed in quotes. 


To the right of the root we give in bold the prefixes and suffixes associated with the root in 


Sumerian. If there is probable reduplication we enter [ R ] to the right of the root. 


Il. 
In the lefthand column we tabulate the Niger-Congo comparative data. Then come Nilo- 


Saharan, Central Sudanic, and, in some cases, examples from other African languages. 


IV. 
Notes are added in square brackets, sometimes with illustrative comparative examples from 


various language groups. These additions are very selective. 


V. 
Finally sound correspondences are given in bold : asterisked Proto-Niger-Congo sounds 
(capitals), followed by their Sumerian equivalents (lower case). Sometimes alternative 


phonological interpretations are given. 


VI. 
The result is a Sumerian Comparative Dictionary. But it can also be used as a partial

TOTEM & FETISH 


Parrot’s Sumer (T & H 1960) illustrates in its opening pages primitive bird-men and snake-men who 
must be based on African totemic ancestral figures. Dead souls could enter birds, snakes, fish and 
plants. There is also a bull-man (minotaur) and a sacred tree (presumably ancestral). Water was a holy 
element. Mountains (and ziggurats) were inhabited by gods. Fertility characteristics are prominent in 
early female figurines, along with marks of scarification (as in Africa). We also find the double axe as 
symbol of the storm god Shamash (which has the same significance as the double axe of the Yoruba 
storm god, Shango), and foundation figures terminating in spikes, which were buried under new 
buildings to repel evil spirits (compare the spikes impaling African bocio statuettes). These replaced 
the people who were once buried under new buildings. The most primitive of these objects belong to 
the fourth and third millennia BC. The beginnings of Sumerian art were African in inspiration, and 


support an African origin for the early colonists.

NOTE ON LANGUAGE RELATIONSHIPS 


On a rough lexical count Sumerian comes closest to PWS and to Mande. We know little about the 
morphology of PWS, but have a comprehensive grammar of Mande by Delafosse. As we have seen the 


morphology of Sumerian appears once to have resembled that of Mande. 


The statistics, based on the 369 entries in our dictionary, are as follows: Of the 369 entries PWS gives 
341 etymologies; Mande gives 331; Mangbetu 316; Bantu 314; PWN 280; Afro-Asiatic 45; Khoisan 
19. So if we take PWS as equivalent to Nilo-Saharan, or Niger-Congo-Nilo-Saharan, Sumerian is well 


up the scale towards Proto-Nilo-Saharan. Our treatment of Afro-Asiatic and Khoisan is approximate. 


These statistics look like hard data. But unfortunately a subjective judgement is often involved, not 
only regarding the meaning of the items counted, but also regarding the degree of phonological 
variation which is acceptable in setting up these etymologies. Perhaps the best way to look at it is to 
regard the derivatives of the protoroot or roots as being the beginning of one or more Greenbergian 
etymological chains, with both transitional items, and in some cases, hypothetical transitional items, 
leading eventually down to the word being etymologised. Either some intermediate meanings or 
phonological forms, or both, may be transitional. We have already referred to the gradual progress of 
dialect features across an area. Likewise “words” (roots) progress along these hypothetical chains, 
gradually changing in meaning and sound. This notion may not be acceptable to a rigid traditionalist. 
But I believe we must accept it in order to make progress with deep language relationships and deep 
reconstructions. Differentiation is fundamental to the development of languages.

THE SUMERIAN SYLLABARY 


In the pages which follow we have appended some excerpts from L’ écriture cunéiforme by 


Bord and Mugnaioni (2002), to illustrate the system of syllabic signs originally used to write 


Sumerian. These signs go back beyond 3000 BC. 


This elaborate pictographic script includes a large number of signs which are representational, 
usually alone, but sometimes in combinations (“mouth” + “water” = nag “drink”). There are 
also more abstract signs, and indeed Sumerian is rich in abstract concepts. In Sumerian each 
of these signs represented a syllable (V, CV, VC, CVC etc) : a “water”, ki “earth”, an 
“heaven”, kur “mountain”. Whole words could also be represented : ninda “food”. The same 


sign may indicate different phonetics : “mouth” = ka “mouth”, also dug “speak”. 


But the script was evidently designed to write, not Sumerian, but Akkadian, a Semitic 
language, and must be regarded as a defective rendering of Sumerian. Thus it has been said 
that we look at Sumerian through Akkadian spectacles. This is particularly problematic for 


knowledge of the phonology. 


The script is clearly distinct from other early writing systems such as Egyptian 
Hieroglyphics, Meroitic, Hieroglyphic Hittie, Cretan Linear A etc. A remote common origin 
of all these scripts however cannot be excluded. The amissable final consonants of Sumerian 


also occur in the Cretan Linear Scripts and in Meroitic (to a limited extent). 


If this feature is a clue as to origins, we are directed towards Westermann’s reconstructions of 
Proto-West Sudanic stem words, which also show this phenomenon of amissable final 
consonants. Probably all these scripts originated in the Niger-Congo speaking regions of 
Africa, which still have elaborate surviving systems of signs and syllabic forms of writing 
such as the Vai syllabary (Dalby 1967). This last has been greatly modified however. It is of 
great interest that some African native scripts mark the Niger-Congo pitch accents. We have 
found some indications of pitch on Linear A cups, Cretan blockmarks and the Phaistos Disk. 
Luvian scribes may also use pitch marks. Even the pitch marks found in recording Burmese 


dialects may be relevant. But Sumerian writing does not indicate pitch, as far as we know.

the University of New Zealand and went to Cambridge on a Postgraduate Scholarship,

Italian theories of Mediterranean substrate lead him to study the work of the Niger-

cast new light on Minoan Linear A, Etruscan, Basque, Indo-European and now 
Sumerian. Graham is the founder of Template Theory, a radical new hypothesis 


concerning syllabic structure and lost sound replacement in early African languages.
