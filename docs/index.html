What is the Indus script, really?

Read it at your level. The facts do not change; the depth does.

Four thousand years ago, cities along the Indus River left behind tiny stamp-seals and tablets covered in little pictures: fish, jars, people, plants, strokes. We can see the symbols perfectly. We just cannot read them. There is no ancient dictionary. Each inscription is shorter than a text message, usually about five symbols. Imagine learning a language from four thousand fortune-cookie slips with nothing printed on the back.

The Indus (or Harappan) script is the writing system of the Bronze Age Indus Valley Civilization, used roughly 2600–1900 BCE across today's Pakistan and northwest India. It survives mostly on seals, sealings, and small tablets. The corpus is large in count but tiny in text: about four thousand inscriptions averaging five signs, the longest only around two dozen. There is no confirmed bilingual, the underlying language is unknown, and even the sign count is disputed. Those four constraints are why a century of attempts has produced no consensus reading.

The Harappan sign system is attested almost entirely on stamp seals, impressions, copper tablets, and incised pottery, with strong positional regularities (terminal and initial sign biases, recurring pair and triple formulae) but extreme brevity that starves any sequence model. Decipherment is gated by four hard problems at once: no securely bilingual anchor; an undetermined language family (Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, isolate, and non-linguistic hypotheses all remain open); an unsettled sign inventory (Mahadevan's 417 versus larger lists such as Wells'); and per-text lengths too short for unaided statistical identification. This project therefore targets structure first (provable positional regularities) before any sound, meaning, or language claim.

The gauntlet

How a guess becomes a finding

Nothing is accepted because it looks right. A candidate must survive every gate below, in order. Most die at gate one. This is the engine that keeps the ledger honest.

Gate 01

Forger & null models

Shuffle the data ten thousand ways. If a pattern shows up just as strongly in randomized text, it is noise. Survivors carry a measured false-positive rate.

Gate 02

The skeptic pass

An adversary tries to demolish the survivor: remove influential rows, block copy-families, let it pick its own best alternative split.

Gate 03

Source normalization

Stop trusting the spreadsheet. Bind the claim to the actual artifact image: token boxes, crop checks, orientation. A photograph, not a transcription.

Gate 04

Blind adjudication

Independent reviewers count signs without knowing the hypothesis. Only then does it reach the ledger, with its surviving objections recorded beside it.

Accepted · structural only

The single thing we are willing to call true.

Think of grammar without meaning. We found one little phrase-ending that always behaves the same way: a specific two-sign unit only ever shows up at the end of its phrase, never in the middle. We still have no idea what it says. But we are sure where it sits. That is one solid brick. The wall is mostly still missing.

Inside the fixed context that begins 002-861, the two-sign unit 533-717 is the only short unit (length 1 to 3, appearing at least twice) whose every occurrence in strict, source-visible rows is a terminal tail: it closes the sequence, never continues it. A positional regularity, not a reading.

Eight artifacts carry this whole story.

The witnesses, the gated targets, the unprovenanced stranger, and the formula used against them as a control. Real corpus rows, real catalogue numbers.

Look closely at the two verified witnesses: they are not the same text. Seven signs and twelve, different in every position except the patina-lit ending. That non-identity was itself tested: exact copy-family collapse was rejected on different plates, lengths, dimensions, and boss. Two independent inscriptions converging on one closing tail is the finding. Sign sequences are the project's real numeric rows; the drawn glyph shapes are illustrative stand-ins, not facsimiles. Where this sits among the other attempts.

The script is having a moment: a million-dollar prize, cryptographic solutions, AI papers. This project treats all of them as comparators to be checked, not banners to wave.

Yajnadevam

Claims a full Sanskrit reading via cryptographic unicity. Not peer-reviewed; contested for an unprecedented many-signs-to-one-syllable mapping that permits overfitting.

Quarantined

Mukhopadhyay

Indus inscriptions as administrative instruments (tax tokens, licences). Published in Nature HSSC. A functional hypothesis, not a phonetic reading.

Comparator

Rao vs. Sproat

The conditional-entropy debate over whether the script is language at all. Unresolved by design: neither side claims proof without a full decipherment.

Open

Nair 2026

A synthetic-baseline scorecard on the same 1,916-inscription corpus we use. Returns an intermediate result, not language or not-language.
