What is the Indus script, really?

Read it at your level. The facts do not change; the depth does.

Four thousand years ago, cities along the Indus River made small stamp-seals and clay tablets covered in little pictures: fish, jars, people, plants, strokes. We can see every symbol clearly. We just cannot read any of them, because nobody left a dictionary. And each inscription is shorter than a text message, usually about five symbols. Imagine trying to learn a whole language from four thousand fortune-cookie slips with nothing printed on the back.

The Indus script, also called Harappan, is the writing system of the Bronze Age Indus Valley Civilization. People used it roughly 2600–1900 BCE across today's Pakistan and northwest India. It survives mostly on seals, on sealings (the clay impressions those seals left), and on small tablets. The corpus is large in count and tiny in text: about four thousand inscriptions averaging five signs, the longest only around two dozen. There is no confirmed bilingual, the underlying language is unknown, and even the number of distinct signs is disputed. Those four constraints are why a century of attempts has produced no consensus reading.

The Harappan sign system is attested almost entirely on stamp seals, impressions, copper tablets, and incised pottery. It shows strong positional regularities — signs that prefer the start or the end of a line, recurring pair and triple formulae — but the texts are so short that any sequence model starves for data. Four hard problems gate decipherment at once. There is no securely bilingual anchor. The language family is undetermined: Dravidian, Indo-Aryan, isolate, and non-linguistic hypotheses all remain open. The sign inventory is unsettled (Mahadevan's 417 versus larger lists such as Wells'). And per-text lengths are too short for unaided statistical identification. This project therefore targets structure first — positional regularities that can be proved — before any claim about sound, meaning, or language.

The gauntlet

How a guess becomes a finding

Nothing is accepted because it looks right. A candidate has to pass every gate below, in order, and most die at gate one. These four gates are the whole reason the ledger can be trusted.

Gate 01

Forger & null models

Shuffle the data ten thousand ways and hunt for the pattern in the shuffles. If randomized text produces it just as strongly, the pattern is noise. Whatever survives carries a measured false-positive rate.

Gate 02

The skeptic pass

Now attack the survivor on purpose. Drop the rows it leans on hardest, block copy-families (near-identical texts that inflate a count on their own), and let the attacker choose its own best alternative split.

Gate 03

Source normalization

Stop trusting the spreadsheet. Bind the claim to the artifact image itself: token boxes, crop checks, orientation. A photograph, not somebody's transcription.

Gate 04

Blind adjudication

Independent reviewers count the signs blind, without being told what the hypothesis is. Only after that does a claim reach the ledger, and every objection that survived is written down beside it.

Accepted · structural only

The single thing we are willing to call true.

Think of grammar without meaning. One two-sign unit always behaves the same way: in this one setting it shows up only at the very end of a line, never in the middle. We do not know what it says, how it sounded, or what language it belongs to. We know where it sits. That is one solid brick, and the wall around it is still missing.

Inside the fixed context that begins 002-861, the two-sign unit 533-717 is the only short unit — length 1 to 3, appearing at least twice — whose every occurrence in strict, source-visible rows is a terminal tail. A terminal tail closes the sequence and never continues it. This is a regularity of position, not a reading.

Claim accepted_struct_002_861_533_717_restricted_tail: within the prior 002-861 branch question, 533-717 is the unique tested length-1–3 unit with support ≥ 2 whose every occurrence in strict local rows is a terminal tail after 002-861. Worst-case forger false-positive rate 0.006; strongest context shuffle 0.0002. It asserts no token boundary, no sign value, no meaning, no language family, no anchor, and no translation.

How sure is sure?

A pattern is only real if randomness cannot fake it.

To test the finding, the corpus was reshuffled ten thousand times. Each bar counts how many shuffles produced the terminal-tail pattern at a given strength. The pattern actually observed sits far out to the right of all of them: a false-positive rate of 0.0002. Randomness lands there about twice in ten thousand tries.

The cast

Eight artifacts carry this whole story.

The two witnesses, the source-gated targets, the unprovenanced stranger with no catalogue number, and a repeated tablet formula used against them as a control. Real corpus rows, real catalogue numbers.

Look closely at the two verified witnesses: they are not the same text. One runs seven signs, the other twelve, and they differ in every position except the patina-lit ending. That non-identity was itself tested: exact copy-family collapse was rejected on different plates, lengths, dimensions, and boss. Two independent inscriptions landing on the same closing tail is the finding. The sign sequences shown are the project's real numeric rows; the drawn glyph shapes are illustrative stand-ins, not facsimiles of the originals. Keeping those two things apart is the whole point of the source-binding gate.

Where this sits among the other attempts.

The script is having a moment: a million-dollar prize, cryptographic solutions, machine-learning papers. This project treats every one of them as a comparator to be checked, not a banner to wave.

Yajnadevam

Claims a full Sanskrit reading using a cryptographic unicity argument. Not peer-reviewed, and contested: the mapping lets an unprecedented number of signs share one syllable, which leaves room to fit almost anything.

Quarantined

Mukhopadhyay

Reads Indus inscriptions as administrative instruments: tax tokens, licences. Published in Nature HSSC. A hypothesis about what the objects did, not a phonetic reading.

Comparator

Rao vs. Sproat

The conditional-entropy debate over whether the script encodes language at all. Unresolved by design: neither side claims proof without a full decipherment.

Open

Nair 2026

A synthetic-baseline scorecard run on the same 1,916-inscription corpus this project uses. It returns an intermediate result: neither language nor not-language.

Yardstick

Tamil Nadu Prize

A million-dollar award, the Iravatham Mahadevan Prize, announced in 2025 for a verified decipherment. No submission portal yet, and no claimant accepted.
